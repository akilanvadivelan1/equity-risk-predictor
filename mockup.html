Every S&P 500 company reveals what is going wrong, in the numbers behind its business (profits, debt, inventory, cash flow) and in the risk warnings inside its own reports. S&P 500 Risk Radar examines both closely, tracks what has changed since last year, and surfaces the signals before the stock price falls.

The Method

Two ways to see risk

The numbers show where a company has been. The risk warnings hint at where it is going. Most investors watch only one. We read both.

The Numbers, looks at the past

Financial health

Signals hiding in the balance sheet and income statement, the story the reported figures tell.

  • Revenue and margin trends, the profit squeeze
  • Inventory piling up faster than sales
  • Receivables and cash flow quality
  • Debt, leverage, and interest coverage
  • Guidance cuts against prior expectations
The Words, looks at the future

Risk factor language

What companies are legally required to disclose, and how their tone shifts when trouble is coming.

  • Brand new risks that were not there last year
  • Boilerplate turning specific and urgent
  • Negative and uncertainty word density rising
  • Risks quietly dropped or downplayed
  • Shifts the market has not priced in yet

Learning

What the filings were telling us

Short, plain English case studies on the risks we find hiding inside company reports.

Target (TGT), 2022
Retail · the profit squeeze that showed up in words first
What changed in the filing

Compared with the prior year, Target's risk language grew more specific about excess inventory, supply chain disruption, and rising costs. The calm, repeated boilerplate of earlier filings gave way to sharper, more concrete warnings.

What happened next

On May 18, 2022, Target reported first quarter results and cut its operating margin outlook from over 8 percent to about 6 percent, pointing to the same inventory and cost pressures. The stock fell 24.9 percent in a single session to $161.61, its worst day since 1987, erasing roughly $25 billion in value.

The takeaway

The numbers still looked healthy on the surface. The words were already less confident. Reading both together gave a fuller picture than watching the price alone.

An observation, not a prediction. We are exploring whether the effects described in the research appear in this company's story.
Southwest Airlines (LUV), 2022
Airlines · a known operational risk that finally broke
What the filing said

For years, Southwest's filings flagged its reliance on aging technology and crew scheduling systems as an operational risk. The warning was consistent and public, but it read as routine and drew little attention.

What happened next

A severe winter storm in December 2022 overwhelmed those very systems, forcing thousands of cancellations and stranding travelers over the holidays. The stock fell about 15.6 percent that month as the meltdown played out.

The takeaway

A risk that a company repeats year after year is easy to tune out. Tracking which disclosed risks are most exposed can help decide which ones deserve a second look.

An observation, not a prediction. We are exploring whether the effects described in the research appear in this company's story.
SVB Financial, Silicon Valley Bank (SIVB), 2022 to 2023
Banking · interest rate risk that was disclosed all along
What the filing said

SVB's filings described a large bond portfolio exposed to rising interest rates and a deposit base heavily concentrated in technology startups. Both of these were stated plainly in its reports.

What happened next

As rates rose through 2022, the stock drifted down from about $559 to the low $200s. In March 2023 the bank sold bonds at a loss and tried to raise capital. Depositors rushed to withdraw, the stock crashed from about $268 to $106 on March 9, and trading was halted on March 10 as regulators closed the bank.

The takeaway

The two ingredients of the failure were both in the filings well before the collapse. The story moved slowly for a year, then very fast in a matter of days.

Grounded in decades of research

The finding that filing language predicts future problems is not new. In one landmark study, buying companies whose filings barely changed and selling those whose filings changed a lot earned roughly 22 percent per year. The reason it keeps working is that almost nobody reads these documents. See the papers below, including two from the University of Washington.

Academic Research

Standing on the work of others

This project takes its inspiration from finance and accounting professors who have studied, over many years, how the risk language in company filings can foreshadow what comes next. S&P 500 Risk Radar does not claim new research. It is a student's attempt to explore, in real companies, whether the patterns these researchers described actually show up in the real world. The papers below are the foundation for that idea.

Harvard & DePaul
Lazy Prices
Lauren Cohen, Christopher Malloy, and Quoc Nguyen (2020) · The Journal of Finance
+

The anchor paper for this project. Studying two decades of company filings, the authors showed that changes in filing language, especially in the risk and management sections, predict weaker future returns. A strategy of buying companies whose filings barely changed and selling those whose filings changed a lot earned roughly 22 percent per year. The reason it works is investor inattention. Almost nobody reads these long documents closely.

Notre Dame
When Is a Liability Not a Liability? Textual Analysis, Dictionaries, and 10-Ks
Tim Loughran and Bill McDonald (2011) · The Journal of Finance
+

Built the finance specific word lists that this project relies on for reading tone. The authors showed that general purpose sentiment dictionaries misread financial writing, because words like liability or tax are neutral in a finance context. Their negative and uncertainty word lists became the standard tool for measuring tone in filings, and Risk Radar uses this approach for the words half of its analysis.

View the paper ↗
Georgia & Arizona
The Information Content of Mandatory Risk Factor Disclosures in Corporate Filings
John Campbell, Hsinchun Chen, Dan Dhaliwal, Hsin-min Lu, and Logan Steele (2014) · Review of Accounting Studies
+

Examined whether the risk factor section is meaningful or just boilerplate. The authors found that firms facing greater risk disclose more risk factors, and that the type of risk a firm describes, whether financial, legal, or otherwise, lines up with the actual risk it faces. This supports the idea that the risk section carries real information about the company.

View the paper ↗
UConn & UT Dallas
Textual Risk Disclosures and Investors' Risk Perceptions
Todd Kravet and Volkan Muslu (2013) · Review of Accounting Studies
+

Studied what happens when a firm increases its risk language from one year to the next. The authors found that these annual increases are followed by higher stock return volatility and trading volume around and after the filing, along with more spread out analyst forecasts. In short, when companies say more about risk, the market treats them as riskier.

View the paper ↗
Toronto
The Benefits of Specific Risk-Factor Disclosures
Ole-Kristian Hope, Danqi Hu, and Hai Lu (2016) · Review of Accounting Studies
+

Asked whether it matters how specific a risk factor is, rather than just how many there are. The authors found that more specific risk factors, as opposed to vague boilerplate, are more useful to investors and analysts. This matters for Risk Radar, because a generic warning and a concrete, detailed one are not the same signal.

View the paper ↗
Rice
The Information Content of Risk Factor Disclosures in Quarterly Reports
Maclean Gaulin (2015) · Accounting Horizons
+

Looked at what happens when firms update their risk factors during the year. The study found that companies that add or change risk factors tend to have lower future unexpected earnings and are more likely to suffer sharp negative earnings surprises. In other words, updates to the risk section often arrive as an early warning of bad news.
