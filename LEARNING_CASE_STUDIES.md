## 1. Target (TGT), 2022

**Card summary:** How a few words about excess inventory showed up before the worst trading day in 35 years.

**Category:** Retail

### Full article

In early 2022, Target looked healthy on the surface. Fiscal 2021 had just closed with about 106 billion dollars in revenue, comparable sales up 12.7 percent, an operating margin of 8.4 percent, and full-year earnings of 14.10 dollars per share. The stock had spent much of the prior year near record highs. If you only watched those numbers, nothing seemed wrong.

The words told a quieter story. In its annual report, Target's risk language grew more specific about inventory, supply chain disruption, and rising costs, describing pressure from higher freight and transportation expense and the risk of carrying the wrong amount or mix of inventory. The calm, repeated boilerplate from earlier years gave way to sharper, more concrete warnings. These were not dramatic headlines. They were small shifts in wording, the kind that are easy to skim past.

Then the results arrived, and the profit side buckled even as sales held up. On May 18, 2022, Target reported first quarter results. Sales still grew, comparable sales were up 3.3 percent, but gross margin collapsed to 25.7 percent from about 30 percent a year earlier as freight, markdowns, and excess inventory ate into profit. Earnings per share came in at 2.16 dollars, down 48.2 percent from the prior year. Management cut its full-year operating margin outlook to about 6 percent, down from over 8 percent. The stock fell 24.9 percent in a single session to about 161 dollars, its worst day since 1987, erasing roughly 25 billion dollars in market value.

**What this shows.** Revenue never stopped growing. It was profit, the margins, that gave way, and the risk wording had already been pointing at the causes: inventory and costs. Reading both together, the words and the numbers, gave a fuller picture than watching the stock price or the sales line alone. This is an observation, not a prediction. We are exploring whether the patterns academic research describes actually show up in real companies, and Target 2022 is one of the clearest examples.

## 2. Southwest Airlines (LUV), 2022

**Card summary:** An operational risk the company disclosed for years, until a winter storm turned it into a meltdown.

**Category:** Airlines

### Full article

Some risks are not hidden at all. They sit in plain view, disclosed year after year, until people stop paying attention to them.

For years, Southwest's filings flagged its reliance on aging technology and crew scheduling systems as an operational risk. The warning was consistent and public. Because it repeated with little change, it read as routine, the kind of standing caution that every airline includes. Familiarity made it easy to ignore. On the surface the business looked fine, Southwest would go on to report full-year 2022 revenue growth and a full-year profit.

In late December 2022, a severe winter storm overwhelmed those exact systems. The crew scheduling software could not keep up, and the airline was forced to cancel more than 16,700 flights over the holidays, on two days scrapping over 60 percent of its schedule and stranding travelers across the country. What had been a quiet, repeated line in a filing became a very public operational crisis.

Then the cost landed on the numbers. Southwest said the meltdown cost it about 800 million dollars, and it posted a 220 million dollar net loss in the fourth quarter of 2022, even though the full year still finished profitable, with net income of about 539 million dollars on a GAAP basis. The stock fell roughly 15.6 percent in December as the crisis played out. A year later the airline agreed to a 140 million dollar penalty tied to the episode.

**What this shows.** A risk a company repeats every year is easy to tune out, but repetition does not make it harmless. The full-year numbers stayed positive, yet one disclosed, long-ignored weakness produced an 800 million dollar hit and a losing quarter. Tracking which disclosed risks are most exposed, rather than dismissing the ones that never change, can help decide which deserve a second look. This is an observation, not a prediction. The warning was there for anyone reading closely.

## 3. SVB Financial, Silicon Valley Bank (SIVB), 2022 to 2023

**Card summary:** A bond portfolio and a concentrated deposit base, both disclosed, both overlooked, until the bank failed in days.

**Category:** Banking

### Full article

Silicon Valley Bank's collapse felt sudden, but the ingredients were disclosed well before the end. By early 2023 it was the 16th largest bank in the United States, with roughly 209 billion dollars in assets.

The bank's filings described a large portfolio of long-dated bonds that was exposed to rising interest rates, and a deposit base heavily concentrated in technology startups, more than 100 billion dollars of it uninsured. Neither of these was a secret. Both were stated plainly in its reports. On their own, each looked like a normal line item. Together, they were a fragile combination waiting for the wrong conditions.

Through 2022, as interest rates rose, the market value of that bond portfolio fell, eventually by billions of dollars, and the stock drifted down from around 559 dollars to the low 200s. The slow decline did not alarm most investors. Then it moved fast. In early March 2023, the bank sold bonds at a loss of about 1.8 billion dollars and tried to raise roughly 2.25 billion dollars in fresh capital. That announcement signaled distress, and its concentrated depositors, many of them connected startups advised by the same venture firms, rushed to withdraw at once. The stock crashed from about 268 dollars to 106 dollars on March 9, and trading was halted on March 10 as regulators closed the bank, one of the largest bank failures in United States history.

**What this shows.** The two ingredients of the failure, interest rate exposure and depositor concentration, were both in the filings long before the collapse. The story moved slowly for a year and then very quickly in a matter of days. This is an observation, not a prediction. It is a reminder that the most important risks are sometimes the ones a company states plainly and everyone reads past.
