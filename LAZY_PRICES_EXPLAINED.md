# "Lazy Prices" — Explained for a High School Student

**Paper:** "Lazy Prices"  
**Authors:** Lauren Cohen, Christopher Malloy, Quoc Nguyen (Harvard Business School & DePaul University)  
**Published:** *Journal of Finance*, Volume 75, Issue 3, June 2020, pp. 1371–1415  
**Free PDF:** [NBER Working Paper](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25084/w25084.pdf)

## Part 1: The Simple Explanation (No Finance Background Needed)

### What's a 10-K?

Every year, every publicly-traded company in America (think Apple, Tesla, Target, Netflix) is **legally required** to file a document called a "10-K" with the government (the Securities and Exchange Commission, or SEC). 

Think of a 10-K like a very long, very detailed annual report card — but for a company. It typically runs **50 to 200+ pages** and includes:
- How the business makes money
- Financial statements (revenue, profit, debt, etc.)
- **Item 1A: Risk Factors** — a section where the company lists everything that could go wrong

### The Key Insight: Copy-Paste is Actually a GOOD Sign

Here's the surprising discovery from this paper:

**Most companies barely change their 10-K from one year to the next.** They copy-paste last year's filing with minimal edits. The researchers call this "lazy" — but it turns out that **laziness is a signal that everything is fine.**

The breakthrough finding:

> **When a company DOES significantly change the words in their filing — especially the risk section — bad things are about to happen to that company.**

### An Analogy Your Age

Imagine your friend sends you the same "good morning" text every day for a year. You stop really reading it. Then one day, instead of "good morning! 😊", they send "hey... we need to talk." 

You'd immediately know something is different. Something changed. The *change itself* is the signal.

That's exactly what happens with corporate filings. The change in language IS the warning sign — not the language itself.

### Why Don't Investors Notice?

This is the crazy part: **almost nobody pays attention to these text changes.** 

- Wall Street analysts focus on the NUMBERS (revenue, earnings, stock price)
- The filing is hundreds of pages of boring legal text
- People assume the risk section is just "cover your butt" language that lawyers write
- There's no dramatic announcement — the company just quietly files the document

The researchers found that when companies change their filings significantly, the stock price doesn't react immediately. The bad news only shows up **months later** — through earnings misses, negative news, or even bankruptcies.

This means the information was *hiding in plain sight* for months, and nobody bothered to read it.

### The Money Shot: 22% Per Year

The researchers built a simple strategy:
- **BUY** stocks of companies that DIDN'T change their filings (the "lazy" ones — things are fine)
- **SELL (short)** stocks of companies that DID change significantly (something's wrong)

This strategy earned **over 22% per year** in abnormal returns. For context, the stock market averages about 10% per year. This strategy would have more than doubled the market's performance.

## Part 2: Why Numbers Alone Aren't Enough (The Limitations of Numerical Metrics)

### What Are "Numerical Metrics"?

When people evaluate a company, they usually look at numbers like:
- **Revenue** — how much money the company brings in
- **Earnings Per Share (EPS)** — how much profit per share of stock
- **Debt-to-Equity Ratio** — how much the company owes vs. how much it's worth
- **P/E Ratio** — the stock price relative to earnings

These are all **backward-looking**. They tell you what ALREADY happened.

### The Problem: Numbers Are Like a Rearview Mirror

Imagine driving a car by ONLY looking in your rearview mirror. You can see perfectly where you've been, but you can't see the cliff ahead.

Financial numbers work the same way:
- By the time revenue drops, the problem has already been happening for months
- By the time a bankruptcy shows up in the numbers, the company has been struggling for a year
- By the time debt becomes "critical," the damage is done

### Why Text Leads Numbers

Here's the key chain of events:

```
1. REALITY CHANGES   → The company starts facing a new problem
                        (supply chain breaks, lawsuit coming, market shifts)

2. TEXT CHANGES       → Lawyers and executives update the 10-K risk factors
                        to acknowledge the new reality (3-12 months before trouble)

3. NUMBERS CHANGE    → Revenue drops, profits fall, stock crashes
                        (this is when most people finally notice)
```

**The text changes BEFORE the numbers do** because:
- Companies KNOW about their problems before the problems show up in quarterly earnings
- They are **legally obligated** to disclose risks they're aware of
- But investors are focused on quarterly earnings calls and stock charts — not reading 200-page legal documents

### A Real Example: Target ($TGT) in 2022

- **Numbers said:** Revenue was still growing in Q1 2022
- **10-K text said:** New language appeared about "excess inventory," "supply chain disruptions," and "rapidly changing consumer preferences" 
- **What happened next:** Target's stock dropped **24.9% in a single day** when they finally reported the inventory problem in earnings

The text was screaming "SOMETHING IS WRONG" months before the numbers caught up.

## Part 3: Why Do Companies Publish These Risks? (The Psychology)

### Reason 1: Legal Self-Defense ("You Can't Sue Us — We Warned You")

The #1 reason companies write risk factors is **litigation protection.**

In 1995, Congress passed the **Private Securities Litigation Reform Act**, which created a "safe harbor" for companies. Basically:

> If a company warns you about a risk in their filing, and that risk later happens and the stock drops, shareholders have a much harder time suing the company.

Think of it like this: if a restaurant puts "May contain nuts" on their menu and you have an allergic reaction, you can't easily sue them — they warned you.

Companies WANT to list every possible risk because each risk factor they write is essentially a **legal shield** against future lawsuits. Lawyers at top firms (Cravath, Skadden, Sullivan & Cromwell) draft these sections specifically for this purpose.

**This is why most risk factors stay the same year after year** — the generic legal shields don't need to change.

**But when NEW risks appear or OLD risks get rewritten in scarier language?** That means something REAL has changed that the lawyers felt needed to be disclosed NOW.

### Reason 2: SEC Mandate — The Government Requires It

Since 2005, the SEC has **required** all public companies to include an "Item 1A: Risk Factors" section in their 10-K. Companies must disclose "the most significant factors that make the company speculative or risky."

If a company KNOWS about a significant risk and doesn't disclose it, they can face:
- SEC enforcement actions (fines, sanctions)
- Criminal fraud charges for officers
- Massive shareholder lawsuits for material omissions

**So companies are stuck in a bind:** they MUST tell you about new dangers, but they know that once they do, savvy investors will notice. Most companies try to bury the new language deep in a 100-page risk section, hoping nobody reads it.

### Reason 3: Psychological Cover — "We Said So All Along"

There's a fascinating psychological game happening:

When something bad eventually happens, executives want to be able to say: "We disclosed this risk. It was right there on page 47 of our 10-K. Investors were warned."

This creates a weird dynamic:
- Companies want to disclose ENOUGH to be legally protected
- But not so CLEARLY that investors panic and sell the stock immediately
- So they use vague legal language, dense paragraphs, and bury key changes deep in the document

**The researchers' key finding is that investors are "inattentive" to these buried signals** — the information flows into the stock price only when it later manifests through earnings announcements or news events.

### Reason 4: Signaling Theory — "Good Companies Have Nothing to Hide"

From the paper's perspective:
- Companies that DON'T change their filings are signaling: "Business as usual, nothing new to worry about"
- Companies that DO change are (often involuntarily) signaling: "Our world has shifted, and our lawyers are making us tell you"

The "lazy" companies — the ones just copy-pasting — are the ones where nothing has changed. Stability in the document = stability in the business.

## Part 4: The Academic Findings (Key Results from the Paper)

### What They Studied
- Complete history of quarterly (10-Q) and annual (10-K) filings by U.S. corporations from **1995 to 2014**
- Thousands of companies, hundreds of thousands of filings
- Measured "similarity" between consecutive filings using text comparison algorithms (similar to what we built in our ERPSA project!)

### Key Finding #1: Changes Predict Bad Things

Companies that significantly change their filing language subsequently experience:
- **Lower future earnings** (profits decline)
- **Lower profitability** (margins shrink)
- **More negative news announcements**
- **Higher probability of bankruptcy**

### Key Finding #2: No Immediate Stock Price Reaction

When a company files its changed 10-K, the stock price **does not immediately drop**. Investors don't react at the time of filing. The negative returns only accumulate over the following months when the problems finally surface through traditional channels (earnings calls, news, analyst downgrades).

This is what economists call "investor inattention" — people literally don't read the filings.

### Key Finding #3: The Trading Strategy Works

A portfolio strategy based purely on textual changes:
- **Shorts "changers"** (companies that rewrote their filings)
- **Buys "non-changers"** (companies that copy-pasted)
- Earns up to **188 basis points per month** (that's 1.88% per month, or over **22% per year**)
- This return is "abnormal" — meaning it's above and beyond what can be explained by market risk, size, value, or momentum factors

### Key Finding #4: The Vast Majority of Changes Are Negative

When companies DO change their language, the changes are overwhelmingly negative in tone. Companies rarely rewrite their risk factors to say "actually, things are BETTER now." Almost all significant textual changes introduce worse language.

## The Bottom Line

**Numbers tell you where a company HAS BEEN. Text tells you where it's GOING.**

The reason this works is deeply human:
- Humans are lazy — we don't read boring 200-page legal documents
- Companies are required by law to warn us — but they bury the warnings
- The warnings come BEFORE the numbers change — text leads, numbers follow
- A computer that reads every word, every year, and compares them... has an enormous advantage over human investors who just look at stock charts
